# No More Excuses: How the Playdate Led Me to Pixel Art and Pulp

When I held the [*Playdate*](https://play.date/) in my hands at the end of 2025, it wasn’t the design by [*Teenage Engineering*](https://teenage.engineering/) or the innovative crank control that fascinated me. It was the 1-bit graphics—this minimalist aesthetic—that instantly transported me back to my childhood: to the Game Boy, to the Macintosh SE where I once typed school assignments. The well-documented SDK in Lua and C made it clear: I wanted to create something on this device. While I had no shortage of ideas and software development was my daily bread, I lacked the artistic component. I had never created pixel art myself.

AI-generated graphics via [*Pixellab*](https://www.pixellab.ai/)? No. That contradicted the spirit of the Playdate, this deliberately limited, neo-retro hardware. The appeal lies precisely in working with your own resources, embracing the constraints.

So, how does one learn pixel art? There’s the book [*Make Your Own Pixel Art*](https://nostarch.com/pixelart), the editor [*Aseprite*](https://www.aseprite.org/), [*PixelStudio*](https://github.com/hippogamesunity/PixelStudioHub) for smartphones, or the classic pencil and graph paper—the analog 1-bit approach. There’s even a [*Game Design Document*](https://www.amazon.com/Game-Design-Document-Development-Experiences/dp/B0FP9P3MXH) for Pulp, a notebook with tailored templates. The options are plentiful, leaving little room for excuses. But what I really wanted was to pixel directly on the Playdate. After all, there’s already an editor called [*pxlsketch*](https://play.date/games/pxlsketch/), which is well-made but whose controls quickly frustrated me. Constantly scrolling through patterns with the B button just to place and then delete a single pixel was too cumbersome for a beginner like me. And analog pixeling on paper? Too much erasing, too little freedom to experiment.

Why not build my own editor? A blend of *pxlsketch* and [*Nonogram Date*](https://play.date/games/picross-date/), allowing me to learn at my own pace, make mistakes, and experiment. A tool I could adapt to my needs and that would grow with me.

At first, [*Pulp*](https://play.date/pulp/), the low-code editor, didn’t cross my mind. Why would a software developer like me use it when there are two APIs (Lua and C) available? But this time, it wasn’t about coding—it was about learning pixel art. And Pulp already has a built-in pixel editor that supports tile-based work. The name for the project came quickly: *Hans-Dither*, a playful nod to a quintessential German first name and dithering—a term I was familiar with but had never applied in practice.

Whenever I found myself asking, *Where do I even begin?* with new challenges, the answer is now clear: *Hans-Dither*, a pixel editor created in Pulp. It starts now. No more excuses.
